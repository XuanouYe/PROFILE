Hardly did I expect to encounter a "perfect" performance in the concert hall, especially with the young Klaus Mäkelä. For me, some of his commercial recordings with the Oslo Philharmonic isquite interesting, particularly his Sibelius and Shostakovich symphonies, with an unusual ear for unusual details in the music, finding unexpected ways to bring them out. Tonight's performance of Dvořák's Seventh Symphony with the Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra seemed to follow that tendency. Mäkelä deliberately drew attention to "strange" moments that might otherwise have blended into the larger musical argument. In the second half, in Bartók's *Bluebeard’s Castle*, that slightly unfamiliar tone found a more direct expression in the constantly shifting sonorities, colours and psychological states of the music itself.

Dvořák’s Seventh is grounded in D minor and marked by dense motivic development, with shadows often falling across its lyrical passages. Even within this tightly unified structure, some voices resist the prevailing mood. Mäkelä seemed particularly intent on bringing them into the foreground. At the opening of the first movement, the low strings introduce a dark theme over a sustained D. The melody begins within a narrow range, then suddenly reaches upward before falling onto an unstable diminished seventh chord. As the theme unfolds, the gathering force is repeatedly checked by moments of hesitation and retreat. In the exposition’s transition, Mäkelä shaped the brief crescendos and diminuendos with exceptionally clear gestures, giving each an expressive character of its own within the longer phrase. The music, with his adjustment with sharp, accented attack and articulation, seemed poised to reach out, then drew back at once, wavering uneasily between advance and retreat.

Those small gestures echo the shape of the first movement as a whole. After the long accumulation of dramatic tension, the coda gradually loses its force and the music sinks back into something close to the darkness of the opening. The movement's overall trajectory and its local dynamic contours mirror each other in a way that reminded me of Sibelius. Energy can build without ever finding release. Mäkelä's emphasis on shifts in the inner voices made me more aware of that uncertainty.

The third movement draws on the distinctly Bohemian rhythms of the furiant. In its 6/4 metre, melody and accompaniment group their accents differently, so the same six beats can be felt as two groups of three or three groups of two. In many performances, those competing accents add up to a vigorous sense of dance. Tonight, some usually less conspicuous inner voices came forward and suddenly seemed to move in directions of their own. Mäkelä shifted the listener's attention among these strands, giving the dance a slightly odd, obstinate character at times.

That sensitivity to tensions within the music also made me reconsider the Seventh's lyricism. The second movement begins with a quiet, chorale-like passage in the woodwinds, but soon grows more chromatic and is repeatedly interrupted by more forceful sonorities. Its lyricism often feels fragile, as if another mood could intrude at any moment. The finale goes further, weaving grave heroism, sudden lightness and steadily mounting tension together, and settling decisively into D major only at the end. Of course, bringing out the details is no guarantee of a more convincing performance. Some of the impressions I had of Mäkelä's Sibelius and Shostakovich recordings carried over here. At the very least, they made me listen to this piece differently.

In the second half, Bartók's *Bluebeard’s Castle* carried that attention to "strangeness" further. The musical material is itself strange. As one door after another opens, the orchestra keeps changing character. Timbre, harmony, dynamics and texture continually shift, reshaping the psychological relationship between the characters. I heard a continuity between the two halves. In the first, familiar musical language became unfamiliar as its inner details came to the fore. In the second, a psychological world already grounded in strangeness and uncertainty unfolded through changes in sound.
