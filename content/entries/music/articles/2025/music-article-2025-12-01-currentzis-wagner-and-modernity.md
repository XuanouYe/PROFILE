In November 2025, Teodor Currentzis returned to China with musicAeterna to perform Lorin Maazel's arrangement of *The Ring Without Words*. At Thursday's performance in Shanghai, the orchestra's cohesion and dynamic responsiveness were noticeably stronger than in the somewhat loose performance the previous evening, 26 November. Currentzis's expansive fluctuations of tempo established a distinctive logic for shaping Wagner's musical images, giving this condensed epic powerful dramatic tension.

As a listener who has followed Currentzis for some time, I was deeply impressed by his performance with the Utopia Orchestra in Munich in early November 2024: Jay Schwartz's *Passacaglia: Music for Orchestra IX* (2024), Mahler's Symphony No. 5 in C-sharp minor, and an encore from Bach's *Herz und Mund und Tat und Leben*. A comparison between Utopia and musicAeterna is inevitable. Even in quiet passages, Utopia could achieve an astonishing, almost ruthless transparency and control of minute dynamic gradations. musicAeterna's overall grain was coarser, lacking that ensemble's mercurial fineness. Although the acoustics of the Shanghai Oriental Art Center's concert hall are far superior to those of Munich's Isarphilharmonie, lending the orchestra a degree of acoustic polish, its sound could still be refined further.

The live result was limited by the orchestra's condition that evening. Yet when one focused on Currentzis's reading of the score and its instructions, and their expression through gesture, his command had the authority of a master. European critics have described his Wagner as an “X-ray vision”. He virtually abandons the traditional operatic resonance associated with Bayreuth: the pursuit of a dense, blended, even slightly clouded “German” wall of sound. This wordless arrangement can be understood as a concentrated display of Wagner's motivic writing. With the orchestra taking on every narrative function, some specific dramatic references and moments of “deferral” inevitably disappear. The *Ring* thus diminishes from a philosophical theatre of power, contracts, and free will into a suite of tone pictures displaying mythological spectacle. What matters, then, is how the motifs arise, develop, and interweave into a continuous, chronicle-like narrative network governed by symphonic logic.

Currentzis handles this exceptionally well. Beyond the alternating display of instrumental colours, listeners are compelled to reconsider the movement of every inner part, rather than simply immerse themselves in the unfolding principal melodies. Redesigned instrumental techniques, emphatically exposed breaths and accents, exceptionally fine staccato and non-legato articulation, painstaking execution of dynamic markings, tension tested at the edge of silence, infinite gradations between piano and pianissimo, and interpretations of expressive instructions that are both faithful and radical: all make the *Ring*'s complex motivic network more clearly discernible than ever. At the same time, the dramatic dimension remains deeply faithful to the original. The instruments almost “sing” Wagner's text, and every change of tempo corresponds to a turn in the dramatic subtext. Even staged performances rarely achieve this, with its declamatory quality recalling ancient Greek tragedy.

Compared with the brass errors and instability of the previous evening, the section had improved by 27 November. In the crucial loud passages, however, the brass attacks remained slightly hesitant. The execution was highly accomplished, yet there was less bite than expected, with something of the brilliance and penetration missing. Perhaps the players had not fully warmed up at the start. This caution avoided the risk of cracked notes, but also diminished the brass's metallic lustre and easy singing quality. Despite reservations about instrumental execution and control at the extremes, this was still an above-average performance. Through passionate physical gestures, Currentzis successfully galvanized the orchestra in the second half, dispelling the previous evening's heaviness. Here I would like to quote a European review: “If Wagner were alive, he would surely come from Bayreuth to hear Currentzis and his orchestra perform.”

If the performance at the Shanghai Oriental Art Center on 27 November was a tentative landing during Currentzis's Chinese tour, the evening of 29 November at Shenzhen Concert Hall was a fully ablaze Dionysian rite. In this orchestral *Ring*, Currentzis and musicAeterna demonstrated extraordinary control, achieving a high degree of unity in the integration of the sections and the breathing of phrases.

With the vocal lines removed, Maazel's arrangement aims to reconstruct Wagner's vast narrative through symphonic logic. The orchestra needs more than soloistic technique—think of the answering solos in the *Götterdämmerung* passages. It must also sustain seventy-five minutes of tension across the long arc from *Das Rheingold* to *Götterdämmerung*. This was precisely the difference between Shenzhen and Shanghai. In Shanghai the playing was somewhat restrained; in certain passages the brass seemed to be starting cold, its tone tight and insufficiently expansive. By Shenzhen, the orchestra had passed the tour's initial adjustment period and was fully warmed up. The sustained strings in the *Die Walküre* passages gave the musical ideas a clear direction of travel. Quiet playing without vibrato was more convincing, alongside exaggerated vibrato in the cello solos. Flexible tempo became an organic part of the breathing. Currentzis's conception of the long line was realized throughout, allowing the listener to perceive clearly the flow of Wagner's endless melody through the orchestral texture.

Where the brass had been cautious and tonally less successful in the first half in Shanghai, in Shenzhen it displayed excellent dynamic range and was convincing from the outset. Horn calls and the tutti passages in the funeral music from *Götterdämmerung* had tremendous penetration, with incisive, unhesitating attacks. At times, a sudden bite introduced a dangerous sense of imbalance, revealing a primal vitality; at others, the brass blended densely with woodwinds and strings in the full ensemble. Just as the anvils sounded and fell silent, we were continually propelled forward. The balance among the orchestral sections was always a dynamic balance of the whole, formed through every section's unreserved commitment.

Currentzis's Shenzhen performance demonstrated that he is one of the few conductors today capable of bringing historical articulation and a grand Romantic impulse into such complete accord. It did more than correct Shanghai's occasional stiffness at a technical level. It attained a freer state, more uninhibited, producing a rare live Wagner experience filled with danger and dramatic tension.
