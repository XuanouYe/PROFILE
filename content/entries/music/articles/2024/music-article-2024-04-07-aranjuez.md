The *Concierto de Aranjuez*, a symphonic work written for guitar, has travelled widely across Spain and the world. Phrases from its second movement can be heard in Spanish-style hotels and circuses, and even in live contemporary jazz. Yet the first and third movements are just as strongly marked by Spanish musical characteristics. From the introduction to the first movement, the guitar presents the rhythmic pattern of the passage pianissimo, in repeated two-bar phrases. Two semiquavers are enclosed by two quavers to form the first three-beat group of the two bars. Players usually strum the first quaver with the index finger, then use rasgueado[^1] for the remaining two beats' semiquaver–semiquaver–quaver pattern: a flamenco dance of the fingers. The second bar regroups the six pulses into three strong beats, D–Em7–A7. Right-hand strums followed by rapid damping produce a short, forceful I–ii–V–(I). The third bar can be heard as a compressed repetition of the two-bar unit. This flamenco gait, alternately quickening and slowing, becomes the texture of the entire first movement: an *Allegro con spirito*, in a joyful dance style.

Such unstinting use of Spanish musical tradition often makes Rodrigo's music seem an authentic embodiment of local culture. The lively, rhythmically charged first and third movements vividly paint the scenery of Aranjuez, which the composer himself could not see: diphtheria left him blind at the age of three. The loss of visual perception allowed him to immerse himself in the musical world he created, expressing inner feeling with depth, delicacy, and sensitivity.

The *Concierto de Aranjuez* emerged from a tragic period. In 1938, on the eve of the Second World War, the Spanish Civil War spread to Rodrigo's home region, forcing him to sell his piano and seek refuge in Germany. Soon afterwards, he and his wife also experienced the death of their son. Between the intriguing happiness and joy of the outer movements, the second movement's almost overflowing emotion found full expression through dictation, special notation, and transcription. Although Rodrigo did not play the guitar, the concerto stretches its technique to the utmost.

At the start of the second movement, the guitar enters mezzo-forte with a simple introductory bar, arpeggiating a chord on each beat. The cor anglais then presents the theme alone. The guitar repeatedly returns to a B minor chord rooted on the tonic, until, almost incidentally, it turns on a weak beat to A minor ninth, answering the slight disturbance of colour created by the melody's single appoggiatura. The first half of the theme rises and grows stronger. Linked by the tonic B, the guitar chords move naturally from B minor to E minor, following the cor anglais's second appoggiatura into the faltering descent of the phrase's latter half. A faint violin sound appears on the fifth, suspended in the air and gradually strengthening. Rodrigo is said to have drawn the second movement's inspiration from a popular local tune in Aranjuez. Its moving, intertwined major-second motif, “down–down–up”, recurs in different rhythms near the theme's end, embedded in descending triplet scales. B on a weak beat falls firmly to A on a stronger one, then repeats almost casually at G. After a brief syncopated lift, it descends sorrowfully in seconds from the fourth degree to the cadence.

The cor anglais's phrase ends on a sustained note, accompanied by steady fifths above the guitar's bass. Across the four beats of this bar, Rodrigo tellingly conceals the motif's slow down–down–up motion once more. It settles on the third degree, anticipating the theme's return, which the guitar's second string and the violin accomplish together. The guitar then takes over the theme in a singing solo, adding many slurs and appoggiaturas around the motif. Syncopation creates hesitation; filling the gaps between accents makes the rhythm freer. Multiple appoggiaturas, triplets, dotted figures, and runs of demisemiquavers and even hemidemisemiquavers weave through a few short phrases. Though still a simple presentation of the theme, it leaves pauses for the imagination. Compared with the preceding cor anglais solo, these guitar phrases offer ample room for rubato, and a much richer listening experience.

Narciso Yepes's 1985 *Aranjuez* recording with the Philharmonia Orchestra, conducted by the Spaniard Luis Antonio García Navarro, bears DG's conspicuous yellow label. The second movement receives an exceedingly peculiar interpretation, its cadential treatment far removed from the breathing and rhythm indicated in the score. The exposition described above is almost freely improvised. Extra seconds are added and repeated in the tremolos and turns, extending the curves and filling the gaps with a surplus of sound. Sometimes the line disappears altogether; one might almost think it a live sight-reading performance. Orchestra and guitar remain closely coordinated, yet even in the rhythmically clear outer movements they often seem to hover at the edge of disorder. Yepes seems perpetually inclined to pluck near the bridge with an exaggerated right-hand motion, an abrasive approach. The resulting noise creates an immediate irritation, overemphasizing conflict while reducing the playing's dynamic range.

If the cor anglais's and guitar's two presentations of the theme form their first dialogue, the second follows shortly afterwards. Once the guitar falls silent, the horn slowly states the motif; the cor anglais returns to the theme in a higher register and develops it briefly. Crescendo, diminuendo, a slight crescendo, then another diminuendo: one hears a slow rhythm of breathing. The cor anglais begins a fourth higher, and the guitar's subsequent statement becomes more expressively complex. There are more hemidemisemiquavers and demisemiquaver sextuplets, without explicit dynamic markings in the score. The empty time seems compressed, yet the intervention of more changing sounds extends it. Apart from the rise and fall of intensity and pace in the final phrase's rapid ascending and descending scales, the response sounds almost matter-of-fact.

Julian Bream, with his background as a lutenist, gives this passage an almost Baroque precision and regularity with his orchestra in the 1964 recording. He largely keeps to the beat; the damping and pauses at rests reveal scarcely any dragging or alternation of speed. The tone produced by the flesh of the fingertips seems at odds with established Spanish guitar tradition, yet it gives the fast sextuplets and demisemiquavers their short duration, matching the soft grain of the left-hand hammer-ons and pull-offs. With John Eliot Gardiner's Monteverdi Orchestra in 1975, Bream offers a different interpretation. The nails contact the strings; the final rapid scale that might call for greater force is played softly; even within a phrase, the tone changes. By 1993, working again with Simon Rattle, Bream takes the irreverence of playing a spruce-topped guitar near the bridge to its limit, almost a desecration. *Aranjuez* leaves performers room for multiple meanings. Even the same player at different stages can produce utterly different results.

As the development approaches, the instrumental groups enter in fugue, growing louder from low to high: first the cellos, then violas and violins in unison, followed by oboe and flute sounding the melody an octave apart. The instruments appear successively, prolonging the gathering of energy and driving this first development of the theme to a climax. Rests then interrupt oboe and flute. The guitar inserts four beats of arpeggiated F-sharp seventh chords into this brief summit, preparing its next solo. The cor anglais quietly repeats the earlier material, its trill guiding the orchestra's residual sound into a gradual diminuendo, until it dies away.

At the twelfth fret, the guitar plays a development of the theme. The motif seems enclosed in circling seconds, then is released into an entire phrase of racing hemidemisemiquavers. The passage is subsequently repeated a second lower in fugal fashion, maintaining a dialogue with the winds. At first, the guitar speaks high in its register, with the winds offering only a faint, low response at the phrase's end. In the third phrase, as the guitar descends haltingly in triplets and prepares to rise a little, the orchestra brusquely interrupts before it can finish. Forced into the lower positions for the following phrase, the guitar gives way to separate statements from horn and oboe: a confrontation of mutual pressure and resistance.

After the oboe's lingering motif slows and fades, the guitar becomes clear again. The bass strings begin to state the theme, *ben marcato il canto*, while softly arpeggiated chords in the upper voice sigh. This low murmuring and these chordal laments carry the whole narrative. Following the oboe's tangled prompting, the guitar abruptly sounds a forte trill on the fourth beat and violently claims the next three beats. Ascending demisemiquaver triplets follow: indistinct preparation, a brief eruption, a rapid release. Down a second, another lead-in, another trill, another release. The tone becomes somewhat calmer. No sooner has the guitar finished than the viola takes up its final note, only for sudden scales from piccolo, flute, and oboe to seize the right to speak.

Listen closely to the responses between the guitar's phrases, and we find that the motif's order has changed. The tonic first rises a second, then drops a second below before returning to the tonic. The brass layers successive versions of the motif fugally, then falls from the highest point to the exhausted cor anglais and bassoon. The closing bar is prolonged, waiting quietly for the guitar's C-sharp minor cadenza.

In the 1980s, Sony issued an *Aranjuez* recording with a newspaper-style cover: John Williams and the London Philharmonic Orchestra, conducted by Louis Frémaux. Throughout the cadenza, Williams emphasizes bass notes sustained under the thumb. The melody has no sharply defined breaks, but follows the score's pulse closely: fluent, tightly woven, and transparent. The clear tone of the spruce-topped guitar produces a languid soundscape in the slow playing near the bridge. After the harp-like passage, the whole slows down. Even the final phrases' gathering eruptions retain more emotional restraint than other performers allow.

At the Berlin Philharmonic's 1998 Waldbühne concert with Daniel Barenboim, Williams plays the second movement on a cedar-topped guitar. Though the cadenza retains its continuous, rigorous rhythm, the right thumb's bass is far gentler. He plays just below the sound hole, fully bringing out the line of changing volume as index and middle fingers alternate. After the suggestive, harp-like arpeggios, his right hand moves slightly farther down, producing a measured playfulness. Two phrases later, without drawing attention to itself, the hand returns toward the sound hole. These minute changes guide the transition between phrases: tender and precise, without abruptness.

Sony also released Barenboim and Williams's 1974 *Concierto de Aranjuez* with the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra. The orchestra's playing maintains a taut energy that contrasts with Williams's manner. He seems to have noticed this difference during the recording, unusually preserving the sounds of contact with the strings and of the left hand lifting off them. More rest strokes and unexpected accents anticipate the orchestra's forceful emotional outburst on its return. The orchestral playing in the outer movements is interesting too. Williams makes a mistake in the artificial harmonics at the end of the second movement: a humorous moment.

At the start of the cadenza, the guitar is extremely quiet. Melody and continuous bass are evenly distributed among the demisemiquavers. Unless one deliberately divides the phrases to alter the rhythm, it is difficult to create dynamic changes of colour through right-hand posture and plucking position alone. The single-note line gradually rises. The melody turns into shorter, more sharply articulated thirds, is quickly broken apart, then picked up by ascending waves of triplets that gather speed. In less than four seconds, the same fingering moves rapidly through ten positions, struggling toward the phrase's climax.

The motif moves into the bass three times in octave steps, apparently undoing the treble strings' brief, imagined victory. Murmuring bass alternates with soft, harp-like arpeggios, leaving ample space across two phrases. The guitar then talks quietly to itself, using accents alone to articulate this short alternation of rising and falling, an ambivalence expressed as E–F–G–B–A–G, E–F–G–B–A–G, E–F–G–A–C–B–A–G. As the alternation continues, the right-hand technique changes and each beat contains more notes. Growing louder, the broken-chord fingerings move from mip–pim to ampi and then pimami, rapidly rising into animated arpeggios in both directions. A forceful pluck of the first string; then index and thumb sweep the remaining chord tones like gasps. Rapid position changes force the barré to relax, producing an incomplete resonance that slightly disturbs this breathing. The hand reaches the eleventh fret again. After a high F-sharp under the little finger comes a deep inhalation, then a breath-held pause:

G-sharp. After briefly holding its breath with the guitar, the lower instruments strike a heavy pizzicato G-sharp. Before the guitar's left-hand barré at the eleventh fret has recovered, rapid strums at the sixteenth and seventeenth frets follow like a scream. Strum, damp; the orchestra strikes G-sharp again. Another roar and strum, another damping. Three repetitions, until it becomes unbearable. These three roaring bursts of rapid strumming end the guitar's narrative and prepare the orchestral tutti of the recapitulation.

Pepe Romero's interpretation is commonly regarded as a benchmark recording of *Aranjuez*, a persuasive account of the Spanish character of Rodrigo's music. He makes extensive use of the room for rubato, separating the solo's varied sounds into distinct utterances through clearly differentiated tempo and colour. Although one hears brief, sighing delays within phrases, the acceleration shaping the larger divisions allows the overall breathing and flow between phrases to remain unmistakable. Compared with Narciso Yepes's DG recording, Cañizares's performance with Simon Rattle and the Berlin Philharmonic at their European Concert in Madrid also contains far fewer exaggerated, startling gestures.

Thibaut Garcia, winner of the 2015 GFA competition, recorded *Aranjuez* for Erato. Before listening, I anticipated the meeting between his relaxed style and the second movement's varied writing, along with the Toulouse orchestra's spontaneity and imagination. Garcia brings the classical guitar's limited range of colours to full saturation, although the cadenza's rhythm is still not entirely continuous. Christoph Denoth, meanwhile, emphasizes the melody's hesitations, with moments when the rhythm slips out of control. He reveals expressions that the open interpretive space of *Aranjuez* might permit, but that the playing cannot quite realize.

Sharon Isbin's 2004 performance with the New York Philharmonic adopts slower tempos in several places. The grain of the attack remains consistently audible. Subtle changes of tone distinguish the voices' positions: astonishing dynamics. Manuel Barrueco's Rodrigo album with Plácido Domingo also takes the second movement very slowly. Its sorrowful character is amply conveyed by firmly articulated bass notes prolonged through vibrato. The nylon strings remain quiet, without excessive dynamic display.

The recapitulation's tutti encompasses passion, solemnity, and everything capable of expressing these complex emotions, so full that they almost overflow. The strings fill the space with the returning theme. Even the distant wind responses between phrases, A–G–A, F–E–F, and D–C–D an octave higher, receive generous treatment: flute, piccolo, oboe, and bassoon realize them together. Each response is like a cry. As the phrases intensify, the waves of string sound seem to have carried us to the distance foretold by the winds. Toward the end of the recapitulation, the flute replaces the strings in carrying the melody. The guitar then returns for a quiet statement of the final, sorrowfully circling phrases.

*Più tranquillo*. The winds soften too. At the fourteenth fret, the guitar makes one final attempt to prolong and retain emotion through a trill that gradually slows. At the cadence, it sounds the notes of the B major triad from low to high. The final bar's harmonics draw the space farther away once more: B–D-sharp–F-sharp, coming to rest on the dominant. The movement has ended as though something remains unsaid.

[^1]: Rasgueado is a strumming technique in which the right-hand fingers sweep the guitar strings in succession. In the pattern described here, the index, middle, and ring fingers follow one another.
