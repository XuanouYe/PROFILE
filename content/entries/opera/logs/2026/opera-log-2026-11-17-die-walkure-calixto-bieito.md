Das Rheingold establishes Valhalla's power through the information control of a data centre, Die Walküre returns to the mythic text to explain how Valhalla functions, creating a certain discontinuity. Getting corpses into Valhalla requires the Valkyries to perform acrobatic feats of transport, alongside mannequins, robot dogs and leaflets printed with headshots: a somewhat muddled mixture of images. Wotan controls the world through digital surveillance and live video, constructing a relationship between watching and being watched. Yet his own space is also under surveillance. Wotan, who engineers the situation, becomes part of a situation engineered for him. The stage space opens up more effectively than in Das Rheingold, with an inverted structure: divine and institutional power operate on the lower level, while confinement takes place above, all within a vast machine that is itself under surveillance. Only when punishment arrives in Act III does this apparatus open up.

Nothung being thrust into the tree and then drawn out is usually the moment when divine design meets the hero's destiny. Bieito's production instead pursues an active, bodily form of storytelling: Nothung emerges from a wound in Siegmund’s body, linking it to physical pain and transforming nature's primordial force into embodied violence. Fire becomes chemical gas, spreading the discomfort of the stage space into the theatre as a whole. After two acts of relative "normality", the robot dog announces the arrival of abstraction almost like a master of ceremonies. The Ride of the Valkyries finally brings Bieito to his most unrestrained, anything-goes moment. The orderly stage space of the first two acts is at last used without reservation, in a riot of grotesque figures. The video, which has sometimes worked against the music, now falls into step with this gesture: its wildest release coincides with the music's. It is the strangest and funniest moment in the entire production.
