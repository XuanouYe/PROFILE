Bieito's recent productions have largely stayed within his comfort zone: garishly coloured curtains and performances that constrain the body's freedom. Whenever something very abstract, disgusting or offensive appears onstage, the audience naturally begins to expect something even more abstract, more disgusting and more offensive to follow. Yet Das Rheingold, the first part of the Ring, actually tones down this tendency. Valhalla's power is represented by a big-data centre, Nibelheim's by human experimentation; the myth of corruption by power becomes a metaphor for captivity under modern technology. These correspondences are all fairly straightforward. There is little that is abstract or difficult to understand, and few extravagant shifts. It is essentially laying the groundwork for the next three operas.

There is a clear artistic intention behind the decision to sever the innate connection between myth and ecology. The Rhinemaidens now wear blue diving suits, complete with flippers and oxygen tanks. They too depend on equipment to exist, controlled by science and technology. Only Freia is free of these external constraints. Resembling a contemporary environmental worker, she is caught between competing forms of capital and machinery of power, held hostage and then readily abandoned. When she is actually seized and taken away, the holders of modern power lose their own abilities. Meanwhile, the relationship of production between Mime and Alberich is also that of modern capitalism, in which intellectual labour is separated from the ownership of capital. This relationship is turned into a spectacle within an enormous mechanical apparatus populated by androids.
