Think of *Phantom Thread*: in the film's final scenes, the characters' emotions undergo an extreme transformation from "love" to "hatred", only to be abruptly drawn back toward an absolute form of "love". In this masterful demonstration of an emotional dialectic, we cannot help but wonder: What is emotion? Is it an unstable construct, one that continually transforms, and even negates itself, through relations of power and dependence? Paul Thomas Anderson (PTA) never resorts to mystification in confronting such questions. Instead, he consistently gives them form through the language of cinema.

It all begins with the lessons of Robert Altman. How did PTA learn to film an ensemble, to present a spatial ecology whose scale is that of an entire historical era? Through overlapping dialogue and intricate blocking, while lending a certain theatricality to speech and gesture. In this way, even figures who appear only fleetingly within the frame can be established decisively and almost instantaneously. Isn't this precisely what happens with Jungle Pussy in *One Battle After Another*? Not to mention more prominent characters such as Deandra and Mae West.

Compared with Thomas Pynchon's original novel *Vineland*, the roles of the mother, Perfidia Beverly Hills (corresponding to Frenesi Gates in the novel), and her daughter Willa (Prairie Wheeler in the novel) have been considerably reduced. Yet within this limited narrative space, PTA manages to render the relationship between Colonel Steven J. Lockjaw and Perfidia, including the former's complex motivations toward the latter, in a fascinating manner. The provocation during the hostage-taking, the sexual assault during the operation, and the eruption of desire in the hotel scene—isn't all of this a condensed version of the relationship between Reynolds Woodcock and Alma in *Phantom Thread*? Equally convincing, yet unfolding with greater speed and force.

PTA seems capable of achieving this almost instantaneously: he renders his characters' emotional states concrete, even exaggerated, pushes them immediately to their furthest limits, and then transforms them into clearly defined entities within the grammar of genre. This, too, is part of Altman's legacy. Yet the magic of PTA's cinema lies in what follows. In the very next scene, he can dismantle these stereotypical "certainties" once again. Through his manipulation of light, shadow, and spatial relationships, the characters' previously distinct emotional states rapidly dissolve, becoming ambiguous and unknowable, only to be reconstructed anew.

Consider the hotel where Perfidia is compelled to meet Lockjaw. The sequence begins with a close-up of a gun. Perfidia picks it up, and we see her standing while Colonel Lockjaw sits on the bed. Only a small portion of the room is illuminated. Perfidia and Lockjaw are reduced to two indistinct silhouettes, their expressions concealed from us. She approaches him with the gun. A stretch of walking, a performance defined by what remains unexpressed. Almost immediately, we begin to negate, even forget, the emotions contained in Perfidia's tears after completing her mission in the previous scene. Instead, we become absorbed in the anticipation of what might happen between these two figures now. Similar scenes recur throughout the film, sustaining a dialectical rhythm of "establishment" and "dissolution". This demonstrates PTA's absolute command of the text as a director, while compelling us to confront the enigma of emotion in cinema.

Thomas Pynchon's novels frequently open each chapter by entering a new, nonlinear position in space and time, a structure particularly compatible with PTA's ensemble narratives and their intersecting subplots. In fact, the first film that came to mind after watching *One Battle After Another* was *Magnolia*, rather than any of PTA's other collaborations with Jonny Greenwood. What connects them is the seemingly inexhaustible momentum of cinematic events, just as the title *One Battle After Another* suggests: battle after battle.

*"Later than usual one summer morning in 1984, Zoyd Wheeler drifted awake in sunlight through a creeping fig that hung in the window, with a squadron of blue jays stomping around on the roof. In his dreams these had been carrier pigeons from someplace far across the ocean, landing and taking off again one by one, each bearing a message for him, but none of whom, light pulsing in the wings, he could ever quite get to in time. He understood it to be another deep nudge from forces unseen, almost surely connected with the letter that had come along with his latest mental-disability check, reminding him that unless he did something publicly crazy before a date now less than a week away, he would no longer qualify for benefits. He groaned out of bed. Somewhere down the hill hammers and saws were busy and country music was playing out of somebody's truck radio. Zoyd was out of smokes."*

Pynchon's opening evokes a fragile tranquility, suffused with multiple layers of poetic sensory experience. By contrast, Bob, played by Leonardo DiCaprio, is thrown into battle almost as soon as he appears. This, too, is part of PTA's fascination. At the beginning, Perfidia walks across a bridge. Filmed from the front, we see her looking downward. A reverse angle reveals a crowd gathered beneath the bridge to her left. Then, in a distant lateral shot, the camera tilts downward to reveal the immigrant detention facility below, where a battle is about to unfold, before tilting back to its original position. Perfidia retraces her steps. We now know what is about to happen in this location, yet the film shifts our attention elsewhere. Bob runs to join the others; a few exchanges of dialogue establish the characters; and suddenly it is night, and a battle begins.

Of course, *One Battle After Another* does not reproduce the repeated temporal leaps of Vineland. Instead, it follows a clearly defined historical trajectory: from the radical leftist revolutionary movements of the 1960s to the undercurrents of neo-fascism in the twenty-first century. This is PTA's first film set in the contemporary world, yet the articulation of political discourse is hardly his central concern. Politics becomes, instead, a series of dramatic elements that correspond across different historical moments.

The sound of the guitar in “Willa's Guitar” begins with the confirmation of Perfidia's pregnancy. It continues through Bob's conversation with his mother-in-law (corresponding to Sasha Gates, an important character in the novel), through the transformation of Bob's character after Willa's birth, and through Perfidia's renewed turn toward revolution. Time leaps forward, yet the guitar continues uninterrupted. Such sounds repeatedly induce moments of retrospective memory, much like the almost imperceptible shifts in space and time in Pynchon's fiction. When Willa is finally captured, just before Colonel Lockjaw takes her to a church to verify her identity, we hear once again the musical theme accompanying Perfidia's revolutionary assault at the beginning of the film. This time, the positions of attacker and victim have been reversed.

Here, PTA pursues an aesthetic path almost entirely opposed to Altman's. In _Kansas City_, jazz functions as a kind of adhesive, binding together the scattered destinies of people in Kansas and Iowa during the Great Depression into a tightly woven narrative network. Kidnapping, political maneuvering, and the intrigues of the gambling world: these fragmented scenes of existence are connected by the improvisatory melodies of jazz into a collective imagination of an era. The music exists both within and beyond the scenes, while live performances by celebrated musicians serve as transitions between narrative episodes.

PTA, however, adopts a far more uncompromising strategy. The score is completely detached from the narrative space, remaining consistently non-diegetic. Sound frequently ceases to be a constituent element of the fictional world and becomes instead a purely cinematic device, serving the formal operations of the film itself. This choice exposes the aesthetic difference between PTA and his mentor. Rather than inserting performative interludes into the tightly structured narrative to modulate its rhythm, he allows the battles to erupt continuously, with an almost suffocating density. An uncompromising intensity. This is precisely where PTA's authorship resides: he makes cinema become cinema, an exhilarating, never-ending struggle for survival.

Just like the undulating mountain road: the least complicated, most purely cinematic sequence in *One Battle After Another*. The preceding genre sequences have already been executed with remarkable brilliance. Yet at a moment when the grammar of the car chase seems almost exhausted, when it has been explored so thoroughly that it appears nearly impossible to produce anything genuinely surprising, PTA boldly places such a sequence at the film's climax, once again demonstrating the greatness of Cinema. The primal act of looking - what an immediate sensory thrill - is manipulated by PTA at an almost unbearable speed. Our gaze can shift rapidly from upward to downward; at times the road ahead obstructs our vision, at others we must rely on the rearview mirror, and then suddenly the field of vision opens up again. We are left almost dizzy.

The fascination with multilinear storytelling that *Magnolia* sustained even during its climactic passages reappears here, now extended through an even more ruthless strategy of "absence". Bob, played by Leonardo DiCaprio, is perpetually late. We anticipate the return of a hero, a repetition of the battle narrative that opened the film, yet this expectation is never fulfilled. Here PTA constructs a carefully calibrated system of delay and displacement. Through editing, he juxtaposes a pursuit that is “still unfolding” with a preceding event that has "already concluded", emphasizing the coexistence and intermingling of different temporalities. Consequently, the rapid-paced "manhunt", which carries the narrative forward, and the slower "search", which sustains suspense, are elevated to an equally intense register. Tense conflict enters into a dialectical relationship with fate. Correspondingly, Greenwood's score undergoes subtle variations while preserving its underlying tension, functioning as an element of continuity that stitches together these two temporal spaces.

Cinema's methods are, of course, rooted in genre. The absurd wedding in the original novel becomes a students' party. The Sisterhood of the Kunoichi Attentives, infused with Eastern philosophy yet infiltrated by capitalist forces, is reduced in the film to an impoverished convent. Many figures are flattened, and even the film's references to politics remain relatively simple. Yet it is precisely through this method of allowing clearly defined figures to operate along the predetermined trajectories of genre conventions that PTA opens up the possibility of an anti-genre cinema. Every arrival of Bob marks the conclusion of an event, a punctuation mark at the end of a scene. 

And so we are reminded once again of _Inherent Vice_, PTA's other adaptation of a Pynchon novel, in which Joaquin Phoenix's Larry similarly assumes the task of disrupting the conventional expectations of genre cinema. These characters wander through labyrinths of time in search of accidental, absurd destinies, only to miss the truth at the most decisive moments. This is also the enduring question of PTA's cinema: in a world where meaning has dissipated, every pursuit is destined to become a misalignment with truth and liberation. And cinematic time itself becomes the most precise measure of that distance.
