As this Mexican film begins, we unexpectedly find ourselves "seeing" a melody. Onomatopoeia, sound in its simplest written form, takes over the entire black screen. Image and sound exchange places behind it, playing out the syncopation of guitar chords. A cheerful bossa nova leads into the opening credits. We cannot help imagining all the possible forms of organization this music might imply: a noisy little romance murmuring lightly to itself, or merely a prelude to the misty narrative labyrinth of Latin American cultural expression? Soon the sound disappears as though swallowed by a black hole. Fade in, fade out: the film breaks formally into something else, an uncompromising game of lines and geometry. Circular objects appear in static close shots; viewed from farther away and at an angle, they become ellipses. A few lines persistently, teasingly divide sky from earth in the distant views. The images are black and white, as though dropped into the streets at random.

Once these still indistinct ambiguities have been established, the film begins without explanation. It is abstract, like a sitcom assembled from deadpan jokes. Enclosed by walls in a medium shot reminiscent of a Chinese Spring Festival Gala sketch, the mother presses the lift button on the left, turns, sets down a plate, takes money from her wallet, takes the plate back, and turns again. The faces behind the door conceal the contrasting relationship among the three people. Her repeated attempts to make sure everything is in order fall into an unexpected rhythm. A light, playful humour emerges from these turns, rather than from the acting itself, and the *Home Alone* trajectory ahead could hardly seem clearer. Yet, as in the film's many funnier scenes that follow, the gaps between these deliberately abrupt words and movements contain numerous possibilities for the plot to stray from its course. If a "trajectory" implies some predetermined logic, what we have here is closer to a disordered, profoundly unstable process of writing. When the mother finally stops ringing the doorbell, her wait is almost complete within the film's rhythm—the lift is about to arrive. We relax, expecting the doors on the left to open, only for the scene's final beat to surprise us again: she exits to the right. This produces a feeling of weightlessness, rather than mockery, like a scattered poem abruptly breaking off at the end of each line. The film keeps observing itself: the characters' comic movements are taken apart frame by frame, humour almost compressed into something we could touch.

All of this language enters the reductio ad absurdum of the apparently casual comic scenes that follow. The danger mentioned in the kitchen returns; the money saved for lunch and cola remains unspent through an act of stubborn evasion; even the stable grammar of genre that makes this opening possible—the relationship between mother and son—is called into question. These reversals pivot on the arrival of two other characters. The girl next door enters the story, intentionally or otherwise. Just as the film's spatial language never establishes connections among kitchen, bedroom, and living room, she occupies the kitchen while the two boys remain in the living room, their conversations always taking place at the story's edges. Even later, when the boy and girl kiss in the kitchen, they hide behind the door and the interior remains divided: he speaks with her, then he speaks with him, separated by the surrounding walls, as though the other two were nowhere nearby. After the game, the delivery rider enters the living room and creates a sticky stalemate. Another game and another power cut resolve the problem, only to suspend it once more. The unexpected humour resembles fissures in a sound field of dense dialogue, through which an abstract light appears beyond the formal severity. The rapid progress of events after these two characters enter the apartment seems to suggest that the doorway will open once again. But Eimbcke offers another surprise: the plot quickly contracts, refusing room for explanation. Colour has been withdrawn so that the images can concentrate on those floating, nervous bursts of laughter. The delivery rider prepares to leave, then decides to stay; the girl is told to go, yet has to remain. The narrative keeps slipping toward unforeseen branches, contracting into an unsettling configuration of four.

How does this group of four become so closely bound when crises keep occurring? The photo album and the canvas extend memory. A pizza set aside and quietly picked up again mirrors an oil painting set aside and quietly picked up again. Repeated words stir the paint before it dries; seemingly incidental yet metaphorical details are continually reset. Time tilts while remaining still, connecting the fractures within and between the characters. The accidental ingestion of cannabis suddenly releases hallucination: disconnected movements are rapidly spliced together, swaying bodies rendered as a succession of linked, motionless poses. Beethoven's Fourth Piano Concerto sits incongruously against the scene. The editing remains strange, another instance of ill-timed deadpan humour. The characters rise amid the confusion, lost in the exile of their senses and entangled in past identities, only to be pulled abruptly back to reality by a consoling melody from outside it. At the climax, this disorderly, whimsical children's film turns completely into a film of adolescent rebellion. Shooting, causing damage: what is rejected is less the private ownership of property, although it is invoked, than the memories of identities and lies that children so readily believe and deny. The "game" becomes real. They destroy the ornaments in the apartment; on the sofa where the video-game battles took place, they kiss each other's ears. The images turn austere, treating this absurd course of events with distance and restraint, as though departure from convention were the route into the unknown. Just as, at the end, a motorcycle carries the oil painting down the road, speeding away from the camera, our field of vision finally returns to the outside and escapes the enclosed rooms.
