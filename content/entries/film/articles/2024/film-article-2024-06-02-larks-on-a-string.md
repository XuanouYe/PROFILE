To the roar of machinery, the camera pans across a rough, rudimentary factory landscape. Sound precedes the comic performance as the characters are introduced one by one. A joking tone of assumed superiority directs our attention toward the familiar figure of the slick, well-groomed bureaucrat. Czech cinema seems naturally endowed with humour. Its ensembles are assembled from apparently unrelated faces and identities, yet we know that behind this seemingly absurd, unstable system lies an unmistakable indication of the political climate. As the camera swings, our gaze also encounters the machines continually working in the factory. Around the theatrical space and its constructed settings, conspicuous, direct performances involving these installations keep unfolding.

In 1990, *Larks on a String*, directed by Jiří Menzel (1938–2020) and adapted by the celebrated Czech writer Bohumil Hrabal (1914–1997) from his own work, re-emerged after more than twenty years under a ban. Winning the Golden Bear for Best Film at the 40th Berlin International Film Festival, it introduced a generation of 1990s cinephiles anew to the Czechoslovak New Wave. In the late 1960s, Czech cinema briefly joined the renewal of cinematic grammar, following the free spirit of the European New Waves. After the Prague Spring, it became taboo once more, transformed into a metaphorical form associated with danger and corruption.

Made in 1969, *Larks on a String* did not pursue political metaphor to the abstract extremes of Pavel Juráček (1935–1989) and Juraj Jakubisko (1938–2023). In 1970, Juráček and Jakubisko used fiercely rebellious surrealism and dense metaphor to construct, respectively, the mysterious, satirical *Case for a Rookie Hangman* and the infernal imagery of *See You in Hell, Friends*. With almost frantic intensity, they inscribed young filmmakers' sharp discontent onto the screen, opposing the authorities' freezing of art with passionate emotion.

The title *Larks on a String* already contains a characteristically Hrabal-like metaphor. Although political "obscurity" became the underlying condition of much artistic work after the Prague Spring, Menzel reverses the familiar satirical stance and fills this film with romance. Innocent creatures constrained by an artificial system: the title already implies the literal loss of "freedom". Compared with Juráček and Jakubisko's work, it feels closer to *All My Good Countrymen*, made in the same year by Vojtěch Jasný (1925–2019). Restrained satire, a setting removed from cities and centres of political power, a view from below, and a romantic manner of writing introduce the ensemble. Nature and humour operate within the utopia presented by the images. Beneath its ease surge the omnipresent pressures of the superstructure. Struggle and the vitality of youth are casually stripped bare, allowed to unfold, and then made to collapse.

At the beginning, the factory workers are introduced in turn, and the industrial setting becomes a peculiar landscape. Its rising and falling levels set faces against the factory scenery. Groups at the top and bottom of the frame are presented like painted tableaux. The introductory speech addresses a silent companion at the narrator's side, rather than the audience. Several endearing figures are summarily dismissed in a handful of rough phrases; we can only wait for the subsequent story to develop them. This silent companion follows our gaze from the camera's blind spot, quietly indicating the suspicious presence of the superstructure. Guided by medium and long shots, we observers seem able to glimpse the propaganda machine working behind the scene.

For the first fifteen minutes, the characters remain in medium or long shot. Like Hrabal entering an unfamiliar setting, the camera observes carefully from the edge, maintaining a distance that acknowledges their equal standing. As in Hrabal's writing, we will eventually move alongside them and enter an intimate exchange between equals. At lunch, the workers sit together, eating and talking. The man and woman who will form the couple are separated by a considerable distance, their subtle expressions after a chance encounter connected through light. Shot and reverse shot bring us steadily closer. At first, the medium shots of the table include other characters; gradually, only vivid close-ups of the young man and woman remain. Those around them have not been quietly excluded: their cheerful conversation continues to circulate through the scene. After several movements, the camera has drawn close, as though sitting beside them and joining in. The characters' movements gradually become the camera's own: looking toward the opposite sex at a distant table, studying the supervisor's expression, and secretly crossing a boundary with his tacit permission.

The camera in *Larks on a String* seems continuous with Hrabal's way of observing the behaviour of his characters from the lower social strata: it stays beside them, looking at what is outwardly visible. Menzel never tries to penetrate the workers' secret thoughts or expose their actual exhaustion. Instead, he catches as many as possible of their romantic, perhaps slightly ridiculous, pábitel-like gestures. Emotions and inner activity find an almost theatrical outward expression through dialogue. The camera joins their communal life, arranging its utopian landscape as a display. Young Pioneers march into the scene singing, bringing the same elementary understanding of the social system. A crisis unfolds in concealment; viewers and readers almost forget what might lie behind these apparently innocent, romantic acts. The missing dairy-shop owner is nearly forgotten. Only a casual question reveals another side of a reality filtered until optimism alone remains.

This compassionate gaze between equals can certainly be understood in terms of cinematic ethics. Filmmakers of proletarian origins, familiar with Hrabal's literature, would naturally avoid subjecting these endearing proletarian figures to a gaze that judges or exploits. When communal life, stripped of privacy, denies all concealed emotional states, every outlet for language must turn outward, displaying positivity and optimism to everyone. Conversely, cinematic moralism operates dialectically in the construction of the foreman's character. We initially watch his surveillance with an expectation of ridicule. Then his affectionate interaction with children briefly makes him endearing, before the film immediately intrudes on his private life: "Is the bath ready?" His sanctimony is mercilessly exposed. A few scenes of bathing and helping others bathe reveal his hypocrisy and corruption. Across these brief scenes, the discourse of cinematic ethics is reversed: irony in every sense.

Following Hrabal, let us quote Goethe once more: only the sun has the right to bear spots. Satire became an important form in Czech literature, and Antonín Máša (1935–2001) pushed it to exaggerated lengths in his 1967 film *Hotel for Strangers*. In this abstract, unfamiliar hotel, superficial logics of signification are abandoned and turned into elements of surrealist narration. A hotel one cannot leave corresponds to Kafka's castle one cannot enter. Indifference, hypocrisy, and sinful lies construct a radically avant-garde, satirical story. The ensemble appears as a collection of absurd, scattered signs, yet the connections among its strange events reveal an unexpected logic. Borrowings from silent cinema emphasize the images' overall comic style. It recalls the old paper worker in Hrabal's humorous fiction: inhabiting a "too loud a solitude", his murmuring sustains the tone of self-mockery and satire.

Kundera might be the more appropriate reference for a discussion of humour in Czech literature. With Hrabal's pábitelé, those unwittingly comic figures drawn from the bottom of society, it is harder to decide whether we are seeing internalized satire or a dilution of suffering. Jaromil Jireš (1935–2001) brings these forms of irony and their associated ideas together in *The Joke* (1969). There is concern and lament, yet the narrative method is saturated with mockery. "Humour and mockery are the highest forms of understanding. Sorrowful events become grotesque events, allusions and metaphors in amusing anecdotes. Everything that frightens me becomes humorous when seen from the grotesque perspective. And from the perspective of eternity, everything is a joke." (Hrabal, "Scattered Thoughts", from *Homework*; translated here from the Chinese quotation.) *Larks on a String* contains plenty of such jokes. At home, the guard chases his wife while the playful minuet heard at their wedding a few scenes earlier becomes background music. Then she disappears—only to reappear on top of a wardrobe. Frontal views catch his puzzled expressions and gestures; a teasing gaze reveals his helplessness. Even the "guard" becomes a ridiculous, lovable pábitel in our eyes.

During a heavy conversation, the camera cuts among several groups of characters, briefly separating them. When the dairy owner—or was it the professor?—is escorted to the entrance, only a tiny part of his body appears at the bottom of the frame. He is taken away, far too calmly, disappearing from the story without attracting attention. Meanwhile, other couples meet in the factory. Men and women occupy opposite sides of the frame; physically close, their sightlines nevertheless seem stretched apart, and they remain under surveillance. A real marriage begins as a contrived, peculiar performance and slowly develops into sincere feeling, entangled in layers of paperwork and political scrutiny. The arrangements for accommodation are controlled by institutional violence. Similar material receives fuller treatment in *The Funeral*, made that same year by Zdeněk Sirový (1932–1995). Czech filmmakers' shared subjects and differing interpretations produce many interesting connections between their works.

Political satire is, of course, a form of humour, and fairy-tale imagery also recurs throughout the Czechoslovak New Wave. After watching the New Wave films of the 1960s, one can return to earlier Czech cinema—the heroic epic of Jánošík, or Jasný's early *Desire*, for instance—and readily see traces of revolutionary propaganda and a hardened industrial mode of filmmaking. A thaw. How precious the New Wave's rebellion and renewal were. Czech literature had, in fact, long contained the conditions for developing avant-garde forms, with its lyrical tradition openly resisting the grand narratives concealed by realism. In his autobiographical *Who I Am*, Hrabal reveals an acute sensitivity to surrealism and modernism. His writing developed alongside the cinematic wave. Through adaptations, collaborations, and the opening of interpretive connections, a corresponding awareness of cutting and montage intervened, almost mockingly.

The visual avant-garde is internalized as a fairy-tale imagination in writing. The New Wave directors devoted themselves to depicting the rich world they saw through new cinematic languages. The extreme romanticism of Věra Chytilová's (1929–2014) *Daisies* finds even more characteristic expression in Juraj Herz's *Morgiana* and Jasný's *The Cassandra Cat*. Fairy-tale stories begin in the manner of fairy-tale telling. Even when their plots seem rustic or unoriginal, their operative logic remains unexpectedly surrealist. After prolonged repression, the sustained, concentrated gaze toward the outside world is bent into the mysterious and becomes our intuition. As Hrabal says, "Mystery is an intensely natural state of mind, a level above imagination: it is intuition."

This mysterious, fairy-tale intuition gives the images a playful quality. Even painful subjects display the freedom of these games. In *The Shop on Main Street*, ethnic persecution reaches its tragic conclusion, then rapidly enters a surreal consciousness. *Larks on a String* builds its fairy-tale character from the elementary understanding of socialism continually invoked within it. Factory operations correspond to phrases such as "this age has crushed you" and "put your class identity into the furnace". The familiar vocabulary of revolutionary romanticism seems so humorous now—in 1990, when the film could finally be screened again—and even in 1969, after the Prague Spring and the French events of May. As the dance sequence moves toward a romantic encounter, the camera's monotonous swinging gradually becomes wild, and the music moves from concealed irony into outright playfulness. What a rebellious game.

The factory setting, its huge steel structures and machinery, supplies the familiar material of allegorical spectacle. From the history of cinema, we know that simply having characters work numbly and repetitively within such a setting can produce a readily legible fable of a vast system oppressing people reduced to signs. Jasný later devised precisely this sort of abstract installation performance in his television film *We*. Its science-fiction "factories" have ceased to be sites of social production, and the film's appearance has moved away from the freedom and romance of the New Wave. Interestingly, in Menzel's *Larks on a String*, the relationship between the frightening machines and the workers seems almost absent. Every possible source of terror is defused as far as possible. Dangerous machinery moves around the workers, both presented by the camera on equal terms. The characters seem to be working alongside mechanical installations, in front of and behind steel and solid waste, rather than performing labour within a set. "Labour" seems to be the sole visual element that persistently exists beyond imitation.

Sometimes labour is accompanied by conversation. We hear the voices first, then the working torsos and the camera's movement slowly carry us toward the corresponding positions. Labour becomes a conscious activity beyond performance, flowing through the rhythm of the images. Even during rest in the communal dormitory, the saxophone overlaps with the workers' rambling conversation by the window—"a bourgeois instrument"—while the screen continues to show fluid movements of tidying and cleaning. At other times, the sounds of labour surround intimate gestures, which makes the poetry recitation in the bunk room so endearing. During a short break, the bunks divide the characters into strange positions at the top and bottom of the frame. The camera swings and rises and falls, moving down and up to present their faces in successive close-ups. Like Hrabal's flowing prose, the film's language carries both the reader's consciousness and the characters' behaviour. The actors' bodies and the audience's awareness establish the metaphor at the beginning of *Too Loud a Solitude*, where reading dissolves words into the body like alcohol. Through labour, the viewing experience flows like breathing.

The only frightening thing in the world is rigidity: hardened, dying forms. Through the flowing consciousness of his old paper worker, Hrabal reaches an understanding with Hegel. Given its creative momentum, *Larks on a String* is still better compared with Czech films made before the Prague Spring. Let us set aside those loud solitudes and take this as a point in time from which to revisit the correspondences between Hrabal's literary figures and their cinematic forms before the bans interrupted these careers. The construction of the pábitel figure already displays the fluidity discussed above in his first published collection, *Pearls of the Deep*. Humour and black humour decorate these characters' unpleasant lives. They talk ceaselessly; driven by an imagination that makes us laugh, speech becomes the sole outlet for memory and fantasy. It amplifies their personalities while reducing the judgments of author and narrator, leaving Czech filmmakers plenty of space. In 1965, five directors—Jiří Menzel, Věra Chytilová, Jaromil Jireš, Jan Němec, and Evald Schorm—adapted *Pearls of the Deep* to great acclaim. In his episode, *The Death of Mr Baltazar*, Menzel preserves many amusing exchanges: a drink in a soy-sauce bottle, the world's finest car, Mozart's *Jupiter*, and a disabled man's repeated jokes and stories about his missing legs.

After *Pearls of the Deep*, many outstanding films emerged in Czech cinema, tracing people's lives and emotions through the minutiae of daily existence. Štefan Uher's *The Sun in a Net* and Miloš Forman's *Loves of a Blonde* film the flirtations and confusion of youth; Ivan Passer's *Intimate Lighting* presents the lives of older people. *Pearls of the Deep* already anticipates these New Wave directors' later styles. Including Passer's adaptation *A Boring Afternoon*, this group encompasses almost all the filmmakers associated with Hrabal before the Prague Spring. In the year of the anthology film's success, Hrabal wrote the novella *Closely Watched Trains*, which Menzel quickly adapted the following year. From *Closely Watched Trains* to *Larks on a String*, we can see the efficient, coherent quality of Menzel's storytelling. The young men played by Václav Neckář in both films possess a simple nobility, and the ensemble writing is visibly accomplished: from the women workers' play at the beginning, through the interweaving of faces with discarded steel, to the heightened emotion of the second half, with close-ups of expressions and hands around a fire, then a high-angle view accompanied by beautiful music. As noted earlier, the factory never becomes a spectacle that oppresses the characters. Only at the final entry into prison does the reversed landscape disclose something strange. A brief farewell; the wife's perspective gives way to the husband's; the factory gives way to prison, rotating automatic doors, standardized clothes and movements. In a low-angle shot, the playful light once directed toward his wife becomes rapidly vanishing dawn, as the swiftly descending lift drops far beneath the ground. Where is truth? Pain and anger burn away in the flames. "I" am born again.
