_Small, Slow but Steady_ was my favorite film of 2023, while _All the Long Nights_ in 2024 left me feeling that Miyake's approach to filmmaking - small in scale, delicate in its beauty - had reached its limits. I never expected _Two Seasons, Two Strangers_ to discover so many new and fascinating possibilities. In filming Keiko in _Small, Slow but Steady_ and Sachiko in _And Your Bird Can Sing_, Shō Miyake establishes an intimacy between the camera and his characters. Watching Shizuka Ishibashi dance or Yukino Kishii train as a boxer, we encounter images grounded in the physicality of the body. Yet _Two Seasons, Two Strangers_ feels utterly unfamiliar. Like a talentless screenwriter writing a summer story in the depths of winter, the camera observes how lonely people encounter one another. We watch Yuumi Kawai, framed in a long shot, remove her bandage in the pouring rain; Natsuo notices that her bandage is gone; they swim in the sea, search for fish underwater. They are constantly attempting to interact with one another and with their surroundings, yet the camera keeps its distance from their bodies. Like the students attending the post-screening discussion within the film, we find ourselves confronted with loneliness without quite knowing why.

A close-up of a hand writing - the only moment we are allowed to approach Lee - reveals that what she is writing is in Korean. This sense of estrangement makes every act of self-reflexivity feel entirely appropriate: the talentless screenwriter, the audience that experiences loneliness, the innkeeper who loves comedies and finds himself caught up in a humorous story, and the film made for his inn, imbued with a faint melancholy. We witness two different ways of moving through space. In one scene, Natsuo is still on the beach, captured by tourists with a wide-angle lens; Lee then makes him "leave" with a stroke of her pencil on the page, and when he next appears, he is already waiting in a deserted cove. Kawai's character, by contrast, undertakes an entire journey on foot, from the exhibition hall through the woods to the cove. The first time, we see the photographs she encounters, the landscapes along the way, and every choice she makes at each fork in the path. The second time, in the rain, she hesitates over whether to keep her promise and go to the cove. The full duration of her walk is preserved.

Just as Keiko's inability to hear others speak allows us to hear the city with greater richness and clarity, _Two Seasons, Two Strangers_ deliberately makes certain sounds disappear, introduces sounds that have no apparent source, and then, after a while, restores the ordinary soundscape. Consider the post-screening discussion: the director is speaking when, midway through his remarks, his voice fades out and a voice-over by Shim Eun-kyung begins. It becomes difficult to locate where our thoughts have drifted, until the monologue ends and the sound returns to normal. Or consider the train moving along the tracks: at some imperceptible moment, the regular, rhythmic clatter of wheels against rails also disappears. Hi'Spec's music emerges, and Lee's journey reaches its distant destination.
